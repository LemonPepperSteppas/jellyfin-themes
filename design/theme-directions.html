Cinematic Glass

The artwork supplies all the colour and the interface supplies none. There is no top bar at all — navigation floats as a frosted pill, card metadata sits over the poster instead of under it, and the billboard runs edge to edge.

Reference points
tvOS and the Apple TV app. Built for a big panel in a dark room, remote in hand.
Best for
Libraries with strong artwork. Anyone who browses on the TV more than the phone.
Trade-off
Blur costs compositing power on weak clients, and metadata over artwork is always a contrast gamble. An achromatic accent means state has to be shown by position, not colour.

Taking Cinematic Glass forward

A colourless chrome layer over blurred surfaces, with the accent left achromatic so artwork is the only colour on screen. The blur budget gets checked on the weakest client you care about first.

It installs as one line pasted into Dashboard → Branding → Custom CSS. Because the accent lives in variables rather than a few hundred !important rules, an alternate colourway costs about fifteen lines.

Editorial Ink

A film journal, not a storefront. There is no hero and no backdrop — the home page is a magazine spread with one feature and a numbered index below it. Posters shrink to thumbnails; the writing does the work.

Reference points
MUBI, the Criterion Channel, a repertory cinema programme. Quiet, literate, unhurried.
Best for
Curated libraries. People who read the synopsis before they press play.
Trade-off
Fewer items fit above the fold than any other direction here — this is a browsing experience, not a resume-in-one-click experience. Didone hairlines need 16px or more.

--m-mutedsecondary
--m-border-stronghairline rules

2px, effectively square

None. Rules and whitespace

Taking Editorial Ink forward

Bodoni display over Libre Franklin UI, hairline rules instead of cards, and gold rationed to rules, active tab and star rating. The serif stays above 16px throughout.

It installs as one line pasted into Dashboard → Branding → Custom CSS. Because the accent lives in variables rather than a few hundred !important rules, an alternate colourway costs about fifteen lines.

Warm Ambient

The inverse of stock Jellyfin’s clinical blue-black. A persistent sidebar replaces the top bar, the home page opens with a greeting rather than a billboard, and “jump back in” is three big landscape tiles instead of a rail of posters.

Reference points
Reading apps in night mode; a lamp-lit living room. Comfortable at eleven at night.
Best for
Households. Shared servers where nobody wants to be woken up by their own interface.
Trade-off
Lowered contrast is the point and also the risk — body text sits nearer 8:1 than 15:1. Big tiles mean fewer items above the fold than a poster rail.

--m-starrating

18px cards, 22px panels

Soft low-opacity lift

Taking Prime Time forward

The familiar shape, built properly: logo-art billboard, hover previews with a match score, and a numbered Top 10 rail. Ships with the profile picker as the sign-in screen.

It installs as one line pasted into Dashboard → Branding → Custom CSS. Because the accent lives in variables rather than a few hundred !important rules, an alternate colourway costs about fifteen lines.

Plain & Fast

The anti-theme. No hero, no backdrop, no blur, no animation — the grid starts eighteen pixels below the navigation and twelve posters fit across. Everything is visible at once and nothing moves. It is stock Jellyfin with the waste taken out.

Reference points
Abyss, Zesty, finimalism, NeutralFin — the biggest genre in the ecosystem by stars.
Best for
Big libraries, slow clients, and anyone who will resent a redesign on principle.
Trade-off
It is deliberately unremarkable, so it will never win a beauty contest. Twelve across means small posters, which is a real loss on a TV across the room.

Taking Plain & Fast forward

Keeps Jellyfin blue on purpose so nothing feels foreign, then takes out the padding, the backdrops and the animation. The fastest of the eight on a weak client, by a wide margin.

It installs as one line pasted into Dashboard → Branding → Custom CSS. Because the accent lives in variables rather than a few hundred !important rules, an alternate colourway costs about fifteen lines.

Immersion

The interface gets out of the way completely. The page background is the artwork you are looking at, blurred to a wash; posters tile edge to edge with no gaps, no boxes and no text until you land on one. Chrome is four floating icons.

Reference points
A cinema foyer, an album wall, the Apple TV screensaver. Made to be looked at.
Best for
Showing the library off. Big screens where artwork is the whole point.
Trade-off
Without labels you navigate by memory of cover art, which is fine for films you know and hopeless for ones you do not. The heaviest of the eight to render.

Taking Immersion forward

Uses the backdrop element Jellyfin already renders, blurred and scaled to fill the page. The poster wall is the navigation; labels appear on focus only.

It installs as one line pasted into Dashboard → Branding → Custom CSS. Because the accent lives in variables rather than a few hundred !important rules, an alternate colourway costs about fifteen lines.

Daylight

The only light theme here, and the only one built for legibility first. Atkinson Hyperlegible — a face designed for low vision — at 16px body, titles that wrap instead of truncating, 44px tap targets and a focus ring you can actually see.

Reference points
awesome-jellyfin lists no light theme and no accessibility theme at all. This is the gap.
Best for
Daylight rooms, phones, tablets, and anyone on the server who has ever squinted at it.
Trade-off
Bright surfaces behind film artwork is a genuine tension — posters lose some drama. Bigger type and targets mean noticeably fewer items per screen.
